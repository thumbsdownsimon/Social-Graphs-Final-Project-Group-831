Crossing the Aisle

Who challenges whom in the Danish Parliament, how they sound when they do, and what changes when parties govern together.

The question

When a change of government makes parties vote together, do their members' floor challenges and language follow?

The main finding

When parties enter government together, challenges between their members fall about relative to chance within a quarter or two, and come back when they split. Their language shifts far less clearly.

Glossary

What we mean by a “challenge”

kort be·mærk·ning noun, Danish “short remark”

A challenge is one kort bemærkning (short remark): a member asks for the floor right after another member's speech and speaks back to that member, usually to question or contest what they just said.

How it looks in a debate

  1. Member A gives a speech.
  2. The chair announces a short remark from Member B.
  3. Member B speaks, aimed at Member A.

B → A one challenge, from B to A

How we count it

  • Directed: from the member who makes the remark to the member who holds the floor.
  • Counted, not read: we record who challenged whom, not what was said.
  • Not always hostile: in 60 challenges we read and labelled by hand, 40 were neutral questions or polite disagreement, 17 hostile and 3 friendly. A challenge measures who engages whom, not who attacks whom.
  • Not included: question time, and follow-up remarks the chair announces in other words.
Not checked by hand

§ 1
Part 1

Coalition partners stop challenging each other

drop in challenges between coalition partners, relative to chance
party pairs that joined or split moved the expected way

When parties enter government together, do their members stop challenging each other on the floor, and does their language follow?

The short version

Danish members almost never vote against their party, so individual votes say little about them. The floor is different. After most speeches, other members can call a kort bemærkning, a short remark aimed at whoever holds the floor. Those calls are recorded in the transcripts, which gives a directed network of who challenges whom: challenges across members since 2009.

We followed that network, the votes and the language across changes of government. Parties that enter government together stop challenging each other, and parties that split start again, including when we follow only the same members across the change. The language result is more mixed: similarity in the same debate shifts modestly in the expected direction, a classifier's ability to tell parties apart does not reliably change, and much of the clearest difference is the vocabulary of being in opposition.

Part 2

Same member, different setting

tone in minister question time, against the same members in a routine debate
 / members change tone between big and routine debates beyond chance (about expected)

Does the same member of parliament sound different depending on where they speak?

Yes. Judged by the words they use, the same members are harsher in question time and in the final readings of bills than in a routine bill debate, and friendlier in the televised party-leader debates. Some members change far more than others.

How we measure it

Tone, and comparing each member with themselves

Tone of one turn

  • Word list: each turn is scored with the Danish AFINN list, which gives words a score from −5 (very negative) to +5 (very positive).
  • Tone = positive points minus negative points, per 100 words, so long and short turns compare fairly.
  • Adapted to parliament: words that are formulaic or mean something else here are dropped, such as tak (every turn opens with "Tak for det") and når (almost always "when"). The full list is below.
  • Who counts: members speaking as members (no chair, no ministers), turns of 30 words or more: turns by members.

Each member against themselves

  • Reference: for every member, their average tone in a , the most common and most routine kind of debate.
  • Difference: their average tone in another setting minus that reference, then averaged over all members who spoke at least times in both.
  • Why: a difference cannot come from which parties or which people speak in a setting, because each member is only ever compared with themselves.

Talk alike, fight alike

Do members fight the people who talk like them?

Yes, mostly because they end up in the same debates. Members of different parties who talk about the same things challenge each other far more than their activity predicts, but inside a single debate, talking alike adds little. Language groups members by policy area, not by party.

How we measure it

How alike two members talk

A language profile per member

  • Text: everything a member said as a member (not as minister) in one government period, with names of people and parties removed. Members need at least words in that period.
  • TF-IDF: how often a member uses each word, weighted up for words few members use. Two members are alike when their weighted word use points the same way (cosine similarity).
  • Embeddings (second method): the average of the member's sentence-embedding vectors from a multilingual sentence encoder, which also matches words with similar meanings.

Two expectations for challenges

  • Activity alone: how many challenges a pair would share if challengers picked targets at random over the whole government period, given how much each member challenges and is challenged.
  • Same debates: the same, but debate by debate, so it also knows who was in the room and who held the floor.
  • Reading: beating the first expectation means conflict follows shared topics. Beating the second means members pick each other out even inside a debate.

The late-night effect

tone after midnight, against the same member in the same kind of debate
 / late sittings that show the drop

Does tone change when a sitting runs late into the night?

Only after midnight, and not every night. On average, turns that start after midnight are harsher than the same member's turns in the same kind of debate earlier in the day, but only about half of the late sittings show it. Before midnight the changes are tiny.

Who do they argue with?

Draft for the group

This part is a proposal (code in topic_blocs/), added so the group can judge it. It is not yet part of the analysis above, and nothing else on the page depends on it.

Members mostly challenge the other side. But Folketinget has two ways of picking sides: the left (red) bloc against the right (blue) bloc, or the government against the opposition. Which one do members actually fight along, and does it depend on what is being debated?

Who governs decides who members fight with. The topic only decides how hard. Up to 2019 members fought left against right: support parties such as DF under V sat in opposition, but fought on their own bloc's side. Once S and V governed together in 2022, members fought government against opposition instead. That held on almost every topic. What changed with the topic was how sharp the fight was: hard on the economy, education and the environment, and barely there on foreign affairs and defence.

How we measure it

Which line do challenges cross?

Two ways to pick sides

  • Left against right: S, SF, RV, EL, ALT and FG against V, KF, DF, LA, NB, DD, BP and KD.
  • Government against opposition: the parties with ministers against everyone else. A support party that votes with the government still counts as opposition.
  • When they differ: when a government leans on support parties from its own bloc, as DF did for V, and when a government spans both blocs, as S, V and M did from 2022.

Which line fits better

  • Topic: each debate is about a bill or motion. We look up the committee it was sent to and group the committees into 9 policy areas, such as the economy or immigration ( of challenges get one).
  • Count crossings: for each topic under each government, we count how many challenges cross each line, against how many would by chance (modularity, week 4). Where there are at least challenges.
  • Compare: the line with more crossings than chance is the one members fight along. The bigger the gap between the two lines, the more one of them dominates.

Methods and data

Why votes alone are not enough

Individual votes carry almost no signal: of cast votes in went against the member's party majority. Communities in the member vote network match party with NMI , and shuffling each member's votes within their own party gives about the same (), so member votes add nothing beyond party. Party-level alignment is what moves, and it moves mechanically: parties in one government vote together. That is why the votes set up the question rather than answer it.

Data

Debate text starts in session 2009-10. Sessions end at 2025-26; 2026-27 has only just begun. Party positions come from vote summaries from about 2010 on, since older summaries give only totals. Biographies were never downloaded, since they hold birth dates, contact details and family information we do not need. Details and known biases are in docs/dataset.md, and traps in the API are in docs/data_recon.md.

Limitations

  • Few events. Six formations, and party pairs within one are not independent. Only 2022 joined two former main rivals, and the 2026 government is too recent to test.
  • The network is rule-based. An edge means the chair called a short remark and another member spoke next. Later short remarks announced differently are missed, and spoken question time is not included.
  • A challenge is not always hostile. The network counts who addresses whom, whatever the tone; of the 60 challenges we labelled by hand, 17 were hostile. Fewer challenges could also reflect what gets negotiated in private, which this data cannot see.
  • "Government" is not the whole alignment. Support parties outside government count as opposition here, though they often vote with it.
  • Language methods disagree. Same-debate similarity shows a shift; the party classifier does not (). We report both.
  • Absence is not a position. About of vote rows are absences. We did not verify why, and never treat them as disagreement. votes since 2017 have no individual rows at all.
  • Party labels. Ministers have no party in the transcripts, so theirs comes from memberships, sometimes the nearest session. Party in the transcript and in memberships agree on nearly every turn, but both come from Folketinget's own records, so that is a weak check.

Provisional and final transcripts

Folketinget publishes transcripts in provisional form and revises them without marking the changes. Its terms require us to say which is which. Each speech carries a status field whose values are not officially documented. We treat Typeset as final and everything else as provisional; that mapping is our inference, based on nearly all older turns being Typeset. Under it, of turns are provisional, mostly in 2025-26. They may change.

An AI agent wrote much of the code and ran the analysis. Every place it touched a result, and how we checked it, is in AI_METHODS.md. automated checks pass, including recounts from the raw XML with separate code. Those checks caught two real parsing bugs along the way, which are documented there.
