The question · draft

Members of parliament do not all play the same role. Some attack, some get attacked, some build bridges and some leave their party. Can we find distinct archetypes of members in the Danish parliament from how they vote, debate and talk?

The draft finding

Mostly one axis, not six roles: the members who attack the most are also attacked the most and sound the most negative, while bridge builders are set apart by tone alone. Defectors and rebels do not form a type of their own.

Part 2 · When parties govern together

When a change of government makes parties vote together, do their members' floor challenges and language follow?

Finding: when parties enter government together, challenges between their members fall about relative to chance within a quarter or two, and come back when they split. Their language shifts far less clearly.

The short version

Danish members almost never vote against their party, so individual votes say little about them. The floor is different. After most speeches, other members can call a kort bemærkning, a short remark aimed at whoever holds the floor. Those calls are recorded in the transcripts, which gives a directed network of who challenges whom: challenges across members since 2009.

We followed that network, the votes and the language across changes of government. Parties that enter government together stop challenging each other, and parties that split start again, including when we follow only the same members across the change. The language result is more mixed: similarity in the same debate shifts modestly in the expected direction, a classifier's ability to tell parties apart does not reliably change, and much of the clearest difference is the vocabulary of being in opposition.

The evidence

Why votes alone are not enough

Individual votes carry almost no signal: of cast votes in went against the member's party majority. Communities in the member vote network match party with NMI , and shuffling each member's votes within their own party gives about the same (), so member votes add nothing beyond party. Party-level alignment is what moves, and it moves mechanically: parties in one government vote together. That is why the votes set up the question rather than answer it.

Data

Debate text starts in session 2009-10. Sessions end at 2025-26; 2026-27 has only just begun. Party positions come from vote summaries from about 2010 on, since older summaries give only totals. Biographies were never downloaded, since they hold birth dates, contact details and family information we do not need. Details and known biases are in docs/dataset.md, and traps in the API are in docs/data_recon.md.

Limitations

  • Few events. Six formations, and party pairs within one are not independent. Only 2022 joined two former main rivals, and the 2026 government is too recent to test.
  • The network is rule-based. An edge means the chair called a short remark and another member spoke next. Later short remarks announced differently are missed, and spoken question time is not included.
  • A challenge is not always hostile. We count who addresses whom, not tone, until the hand labels are in. Fewer challenges could also reflect what gets negotiated in private, which this data cannot see.
  • "Government" is not the whole alignment. Support parties outside government count as opposition here, though they often vote with it.
  • Language methods disagree. Same-debate similarity shows a shift; the party classifier does not (). We report both.
  • Absence is not a position. About of vote rows are absences. We did not verify why, and never treat them as disagreement. votes since 2017 have no individual rows at all.
  • Party labels. Ministers have no party in the transcripts, so theirs comes from memberships, sometimes the nearest session. Party in the transcript and in memberships agree on nearly every turn, but both come from Folketinget's own records, so that is a weak check.

Provisional and final transcripts

Folketinget publishes transcripts in provisional form and revises them without marking the changes. Its terms require us to say which is which. Each speech carries a status field whose values are not officially documented. We treat Typeset as final and everything else as provisional; that mapping is our inference, based on nearly all older turns being Typeset. Under it, of turns are provisional, mostly in 2025-26. They may change.

How we checked our own work

An AI agent wrote much of the code and ran the analysis. Every place it touched a result, and how we checked it, is in AI_METHODS.md. automated checks pass, including recounts from the raw XML with separate code. Those checks caught two real parsing bugs along the way, which are documented there.
